The Outward Transform

Everything published in this repository passed through a transform on its way out of the private working corpus. This document says exactly what that transform does, what it does not do, and what an item must satisfy before it ships.

What the transform does — and does not do

Every file that crosses into this public copy is de-named: the owner's name, in every form, is mechanically replaced with the role-word "the keeper," and usernames, machine paths, and private locations are replaced with placeholders. The transform is deterministic and runs on copies only — the private corpus keeps its full, named record.

What that is: a reading convention. It marks, on every page, which way the work faces — outward, as pattern to pick up, not as biography — and it takes the author's name out of the position of authority, so nothing here can be adopted because of who wrote it.

What that is not: anonymity. Removing a name does not remove an identity — writing style alone identifies authors at internet scale, and this repository quotes its owner's decisions verbatim throughout. The personal essays here are recognizably about a specific life, on purpose. Anyone who needs actual anonymity should know this transform would not provide it, and it does not claim to.

The landing-clean checklist

De-naming is the smallest part of making work usable by a stranger. An item ships outward only when all six lines hold:

  1. De-named — the transform has run, verified by scan, not intention.
  2. Jargon glossed or shed — every term of art the item leans on is defined where it's used, linked to its definition, or removed. A stranger reads it without a glossary they don't have.
  3. Tacit context stated — what the item assumes about its situation (scale, who holds what, what already exists) is written down. The great cautionary tale is the famous scholar's note-box: sixty thousand notes, and a funded university team has spent over a decade making them usable, because the barrier was never the name on them — it was the context that stayed in the author's head.
  4. Standalone-usable — the item works without adopting the rest of this project: opened, read, or run on its own ground.
  5. Provenance without dependence — the item says where it came from, so it can be traced, but nothing in it requires the reader to go back upstream.
  6. Its test travels with it — the item states, inside itself, what would show it wrong.

An item that fails a line does not ship; it goes back to the workshop with the failing line named. There is no partial crossing.

How to show this wrong: hand any published item to a reader with no context and ask them to use it. Every question they must ask before it works is a checklist line that was signed off and did not hold.
