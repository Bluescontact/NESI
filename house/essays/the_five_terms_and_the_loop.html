Systems Thinking / Vocabulary

Five Words, One Shape

A working vocabulary for systems thinkers — five terms, five tests, one loop.

Here's what five words actually get you: the ability to tell, about a policy, a relationship, a system you're mid-build on, what's really holding it up and what's just standing there — before you find out the hard way, under pressure, that the thing you were counting on was never load-bearing at all.

Framing, Load-bearing, Composting, Gate, Container. Not five definitions to memorize — five two-minute checks you run yourself, on your own material, on whatever's in front of you right now. Run all five on the same thing and you don't get five separate readings. You get a map: what shaped what you noticed, what's actually structural, what happens to the pieces that fail, what actually stops something versus what only asks nicely, and where it's supposed to end. That map is the product. Whether the five add up to one loop or just five useful checks that happen to reference each other is a real question, and it gets a real answer, limit included, once all five are on the table.

Term One

Framing

Definition. The angle a true statement is cast in — the same fact, delivered through a different lens, producing a different downstream response. It doesn't change what happened. It changes what the listener does next.
The testRewrite a fact-carrying sentence, changing only the frame, holding the fact constant. Read both versions yourself, back to back, and notice what moves in your own body before you form an opinion about either — where the urgency lands, whether you feel permission or obligation, what you'd actually do in the next five minutes if it were said to you. If nothing shifts, the frame wasn't doing anything. If something does, that shift is framing's weight — independent of which version is more truthful.

"Employees are not required to justify time off" versus "Employees are trusted to manage their own time off." Same policy, zero difference in what's allowed, two different feelings about asking for a Friday off.

Where it failsThe moment a frame is asked to change the fact underneath it, not your relationship to it. A frame that survives you checking the fact yourself, once the felt shift has worn off, is framing. One that collapses the moment you check it on your own — that made something harmful sound optional — is concealment wearing a frame's clothes.

A frame matters only once you ask whether it's load-bearing: does removing it change something real, or just the tone?

Term Two

Load-bearing

Definition. Structure or language that, if removed, causes something else to fail — as against decorative material, which can be removed with nothing downstream changing.
The testThe subtraction test. Remove the piece — on paper if not literally — and trace forward. Does anything that depended on it now fail, at some real distance downstream? If yes, load-bearing. If nothing moves, decorative — not an insult, decoration has its own job, just not this one.

A "documented warning before termination" clause in a performance-review process: pull it and trace forward — what evidence exists later, what a dispute can point to, whether the employee ever got a signal before the consequence landed. If those actually change, it was load-bearing. A clause that just restates something covered elsewhere fails the same trace: pull it, nothing moves.

Where it failsTreating frequency, tenure, or how uncomfortable removal feels as evidence. None of that is the test — only the subtraction is. Defending something as load-bearing because removing it feels bad, not because removing it breaks anything, is nostalgia arguing in structure's name.

Once the subtraction test says "nothing failed," the thing isn't worth defending anymore — it's free material. What happens to something that just failed its load test is the next term's subject.

Term Three

Composting

Definition. Treating a failure as raw material to recombine into something new — rather than preserving it untouched out of attachment, or discarding it as simply wrong and closing the file.
The testCan you name one specific place, downstream, where a piece of the failed thing is now doing real work in a different combination? If yes, composting happened. If the honest answer is a vague feeling of having "learned something," it didn't.

A failed feature gets killed. Deleting the ticket and never mentioning it again is one failure — the lesson gets relearned in eighteen months. Pinning the whole spec in a graveyard doc nobody rereads is the same failure, slower. What's different: extract the one specific reason it failed and route that finding into the next three designs, whatever they look like. The feature died. The finding didn't.

Where it fails"There are no failures, only lessons" sounds like the concept and does none of its work — a thought-terminator, not an extraction. Name the transferable piece and where it landed, or it didn't happen.

Composting only works if something decides what's actually allowed back in, and where. What decides — mechanically, not by vote or mood — is the next term.

Term Four

Gate

Definition. A checkpoint that mechanically blocks or permits something, independent of whether anyone remembers or agrees with the rule — as against a rule that only holds because someone chooses, moment to moment, to honor it.
The testThe ignore test. Imagine everyone simultaneously forgets the rule, or wants around it, right now. Does the outcome still get blocked? If yes, gate. If it depends on someone choosing to comply, rule. Rules aren't worthless — most of ethics is rules — but a rule and a gate fail in opposite ways, and mistaking one for the other is how a boundary turns out to have never been load-bearing, at the worst possible moment.

"Please don't share your login" is a rule. A login form that won't accept a request without a matching password is a gate — indifferent to who's asking. But that indifference is physics-borrowed language applied to something people built: a human-maintained gate is a gate only as long as the people maintaining it keep it one. It can be loosened or overridden in a way a lock cannot. Rules still hold real ground: Princeton ran unproctored exams on an honor code alone, no proctor at the door, for over a century — a container that held without a gate at its edge, on shared stakes rather than mechanism. (It ended that system in 2026, under different pressures entirely; a century holding is still a century, worth naming exactly, not skated past.)

Where it failsGate-washing — calling something a gate to borrow its credibility while it's actually a rule. "Requires sign-off" that still ships without the sign-off was always a rule wearing a gate's name. The opposite failure is real too: a hard gate on something that occasionally needs judgment is brittle, not resilient.

A gate only means something at an edge. Every real gate implies a boundary — the last term.

Term Five

Container

Definition. The boundary of a system or conversation — what's inside, and just as importantly, what's been deliberately placed outside, rather than simply never mentioned.
The testCan you point to where the boundary sits, and name one thing deliberately outside it? "It covers everything relevant" means there's no container yet — just an area that's never been asked to end.

"Budget review" with no stated container invites the conversation wherever gravity pulls it. "Budget review: Q3 actuals versus plan only — staffing is a separate item, not today" named what's out, not just what's in. That's the half doing the actual work.

Where it failsA container stated once and never enforced is just a sentence — which is why it needs a real gate at its edge, or it degrades into an aspiration the second time it's tested. The opposite failure: a container so rigid it excludes the thing that should have triggered a deliberate redraw.

What gets treated as inside a container is itself a framing choice — which closes the loop.

The Whole

Five Words, One Loop — and Its Limit

Framing decides what's noticed. What's noticed gets tested for whether it's load-bearing, by subtraction. What fails gets composted — broken into pieces true somewhere else. A gate decides, mechanically, what composted material re-enters, and where. What's inside the gate is a container, with a nameable edge. And the container is now a fact, available to be framed — where the loop began. Each term catches a failure its neighbor is prone to when that neighbor is run carelessly, or performed instead of actually done.

Here's the limit, and it's load-bearing to the whole claim, not an aside: across the missions before Challenger, NASA's own Flight Readiness Review — a real, mechanical sign-off gate — repeatedly saw evidence of O-ring erosion on prior flights and repeatedly signed off on it as "within acceptable risk," each time re-certifying a conclusion nobody with fresh eyes re-examined. A sociologist studying the disaster named this pattern normalization of deviance. On the night before the January 1986 launch, engineers who raised a late, specific warning about cold-temperature O-ring risk were overruled by managers under schedule pressure — a rule bending under pressure, exactly the failure this piece already named. But the deeper failure sits earlier: the gate itself, working exactly as a gate, had already spent two years certifying the same risk as acceptable, one signed-off flight at a time.

A gate can verify a sign-off happened. It cannot verify that what was signed off was true — that's what makes it a gate rather than a rule, and the same indifference that makes it one is what makes it blind to content. So the honest claim is smaller than "these five terms form a self-correcting loop": the loop catches the failure that comes from skipping, faking, or performing a step. It cannot catch a step done carefully, in good faith, whose content was simply wrong. For that failure, the loop needs something outside itself — a second, disinterested set of eyes.

Where This Leaves You

The Part That Holds the Whole

Five questions, not an answer: What's this framed as, and does it survive you checking the fact yourself, once the felt shift has worn off? What's actually load-bearing here, by subtraction, versus merely present? What already failed once, and where did the useful piece get routed, if anywhere? What's a gate, and what's only ever been a rule wearing a gate's reputation? And where, exactly, does this end — at an edge someone could point to, not by drift?

Run those on anything — this piece included — and what you get back is the map promised at the start: which parts are load-bearing and which are decoration, which boundary is a real gate and which is a hope wearing one's name, where the thing was always going to end once someone finally named the edge. That map is the actual output — not a verdict on whether the thing is good, a diagram of what's producing it. The one honest gap in it is the size it's always been: the map can be careful, complete, and still miss the failure that was carried out carefully and wrong, the way the Flight Readiness Review's map did. Which is why the map is where you start checking something, never where you stop.
