The Hunger Under Naming

A reader wrote in after "Say the Names" with a lens the essay hadn't drawn on, and the contribution was absorbed at mechanism level — not as a new name for the essay's list, but as a mechanism running underneath the naming project itself. In the working version of this piece the contribution is credited by name; here, per the commons rule, only the pattern crosses.

The reader did two things a reply rarely does in one note. They connected a word the essay had used narrowly — "scapegoat," inside one paragraph about corporate prosecutions — to a whole body of theory on imitative desire the essay hadn't touched. And they were honest that they were still working out what it meant themselves, rather than arriving with a finished thesis to defend. That's the useful kind of reply: it hands over a real lead and lets the piece do its own checking, instead of asking to be agreed with.

The actual mechanism

The theory's claim is narrower and stranger than the pop version, so it's worth getting right before using it. Desire, in this account, is triangular: we want things because a model wants them, not because the things are intrinsically wanted. Imitation escalates into mimetic rivalry, rivalry spreads through a group as contagion, and the resulting crisis resolves when collective violence converges on a victim chosen not for actual guilt but for being available — marginal, different, disposable. Expelling that victim produces real peace, which the group misreads as the victim's own dark power, sacred and cursed at once. Ritual repeats the killing afterward, to manage the next crisis before it arrives. This is the scapegoat mechanism proper — not a synonym for unfair blame, but a specific machine for converting group crisis into group peace.

What it changes in the lens

Here's what actually moved, reading the note: the essay had been treating misnaming — "humanity," a scapegoated line employee instead of the executive who set the incentive — as a correctable error. Look closer, cite better, and the naming gets more accurate. The theory's claim is darker than that, and it's the kind of claim you don't want to be right. If a group needs the discharge more than it needs the truth, then getting the name correct doesn't obviously stop the mechanism — it just makes the next round harder to disguise as justice. The essay had spent a fair amount of effort on precision as the fix. This note was the first thing that forced the question of whether precision is a fix at all, or just a better-dressed version of the same hunger.

And there's a sharper version of that question the essay can't fully answer about itself: is the habit of naming — one figure after another, in the essay's own lineage — actually reached by evidence, the way it claims, or does some of its satisfaction come from somewhere older than the argument? The essay's defense is structural: every name in it was reached by following a document to a signature, evidence first. The scapegoat mechanism's victim is reached by consensus first, guilt assembled after — the opposite direction. That's a real defense. It is not a guarantee, and the honest move is to hold the discomfort of that rather than resolve it too fast.

What it changes in the lens as held

The essay's standing claim doesn't change: name the decision where a decision was made, name the mechanism where the harm is structural instead, and stop reaching for "humanity" to avoid doing either. That argument survives the note intact. What the held lens gains is a caveat it didn't earn on its own — that naming correctly is not the same thing as naming safely, and the essay's optimism about accountability needs that qualifier sitting next to it from now on, not as a footnote but as part of the claim.

It's worth being precise about where the addition stops, too, so the lineage stays as honest as the essay asks everything else to be. The scapegoat mechanism doesn't extend the essay's event/structure distinction — the essay's "structure" is about how harm gets produced; this mechanism is about how blame gets allocated — and merging them would make the lineage tidier and less true. The reader's note also floated a further claim, that this body of theory shaped the design of social-media algorithms. What's documented is influence at the level of individual admirers and funded institutions; a direct line into algorithm design is not documented, and would need its own memo and its own named decision-maker before it earned a place on any list held to this essay's standard. And the theory's harshest critics — arguing it is unfalsifiable and forces ethnographically diverse practices into one origin myth — would say it can't carry that kind of weight for any case, this one included.

So: no new name on the list. One real addition — the mechanism running underneath the naming project itself — contributed from outside, absorbed at mechanism level, and held now as part of what the essay knows about its own method.
