# Match the Noun to the Harm

*A reply to a real question: if "humanity" is disconnected from Gaia, who — specifically — did the disconnecting? And what does it mean to research a repair?*

---

This piece began as a correction. An earlier draft said that humanity is being held accountable for its disconnection from Gaia, and a careful reader pushed back: that sentence generalizes, and the generalization does a specific job — it shields the actual actors doing the harm by dissolving them into a population that also includes the people who never disconnected, who kept the old ways, who never gave them up under pressure or after. Those people are humanity too. Charging "humanity" charges them along with everyone else, at the exact moment precision was owed.

The reader was right, and the fix isn't a better adjective. It's naming — though, as it turned out, naming wasn't quite the whole fix either. That took longer to find.

One thing to say plainly at the outset, because it is part of this argument's honesty rather than an apology for it: this essay argues *for* naming decision-makers, and the working version of it does name them — the philosopher, the executives, the scientists, the scholars, each with the receipt attached. This commons copy carries the full mechanism and the shape of every receipt — the dates, the institutions, the findings — and deliberately not the names, because what crosses into the commons is pattern, never instance. The method survives the substitution intact; where the working version says a name, this one says the role and the decision, which is the load-bearing part anyway.

## Why the blur happens

"Humanity" is the easiest noun in the English language to reach for when you want to describe a planetary-scale wrong and don't want to be the one who has to point. It sounds humble — *we're all in this together, we all did this* — and humility is exactly the texture that lets it pass unexamined. But there's a difference between *the harm reaches everyone* and *everyone caused the harm*, and the word "humanity," used carelessly, collapses that difference on purpose. A named actor can at least be studied, opposed, and — sometimes, over time, against resistance — held to account. A diffuse species-level guilt sits beyond the reach of any of that; there's no file to open on it, no hearing to call.

So: who.

## The actual list

Not a list of individuals to hate. A list of specific doctrines, instruments, and institutions that did specific work, at specific times, staffed by people whose identities are, in most of these cases, a matter of public record — which is exactly why the working version can name them and this copy doesn't need to.

**The mind-body split, and the mandate to interrogate nature.** A seventeenth-century philosopher gave the West a metaphysics in which mind and matter are two different substances, and only mind carries value. Everything that is *merely* matter — animals, rivers, forests, the body itself — became, by definition, a machine without an inside. That claim doesn't need a vivid illustration to stand; it's the load-bearing one. An early architect of the experimental method supplied the illustration anyway: nature was something to be put "in constraint," made to "confess" its secrets. A historian of science's 1980 study, *The Death of Nature*, reads that as nature bent to human purpose the way a prisoner is bent under interrogation; other historians of science read the same lines as inherited legal metaphor, nothing more sinister. That dispute doesn't need resolving here — the interrogation language is color, not cargo. The cargo is the metaphysics: a civilization that has already defined the living world as valueless matter has, by that same stroke, given itself permission to treat it as inert stock. You cannot disconnect from something you've already defined as having nothing to connect to.

**Terra nullius and the doctrine of discovery.** Two related but distinct instruments, worth separating rather than blurring. The doctrine of discovery traces to a specific set of 15th-century papal bulls — *Romanus Pontifex* (1455), *Inter Caetera* (1493) — that authorized Christian powers to claim land held by non-Christian peoples. Terra nullius is the later doctrine, built on Roman *res nullius*, that treated land as legally unoccupied regardless of who was standing on it; its most consequential undoing came in 1992, when the Australian High Court struck down its application to a continent that had never, in fact, been empty. Different instruments, different centuries, one throughline: each converted relationship into property in a single legal stroke, and each is still cited — or being unwound — in land-title case law today.

**Enclosure.** Centuries of Enclosure Acts in Britain, and their exported equivalents across colonized land, took commons — land held and tended in shared, reciprocal relationship — and converted them into private, fenced, individually owned parcels. This did two things at once: it severed people from land they had a working relationship with, and it severed land from any obligation to be *related to* at all, since a fenced parcel answers to an owner's ledger, not to a community's reciprocity. Enclosure is the mechanism, not the metaphor, behind "no one owns the river, so no one is accountable to it."

**The externality, as an accounting category.** Modern economics has a formal term — externality — for a cost a transaction produces but does not require anyone in the transaction to pay. In standard national accounts, a river poisoned downstream of a factory is worth exactly zero. This isn't hidden or disputed; it's how the accounting was built. The economist who designed the national-income framework GDP was later assembled from delivered it to Congress in 1934 — and in that same report, warned against the very use it would be put to: "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income." He lost that fight inside his own report; the version of the accounting that prevailed dropped the welfare-sensitive subtractions he had argued for, and the measure ran forward through GNP and then GDP for the rest of the century, alongside a marginalized but real countertradition of alternative accounting (the UN's environmental-economic accounts, various "green GDP" efforts) that never displaced it. The externality is a design choice with an author on record warning against the use it was put to, inside a fight he didn't win.

**The fossil-fuel disinformation architecture.** The clearest paper trail of the six. In 1977, a senior scientist inside Exxon told the company's own management committee that its product would warm the planet — and he was right, by the modeling standards of the time. That's internal research, done by the company's own people, reaching an accurate conclusion. What followed was not the same organization saying something different; it was a separate apparatus — the American Petroleum Institute, the Global Climate Coalition, a network of funded think tanks — spending the next several decades manufacturing public doubt about a conclusion the company's own scientists had already reached internally. Naming this precisely matters: it wasn't one unified actor with two faces, it was a research arm and a lobbying-and-PR arm, distinct organizations, coordinated toward the same end. Naming it has not, so far, stopped it — the trade association and its member companies are still lobbying against binding emissions law today, nearly fifty years after that internal briefing. The naming was necessary. It has not, on its own, been sufficient.

**The ideology of infinite growth on a finite planet.** This one sits differently than the others, and it's worth saying so rather than smoothing over it. Unlike a papal bull or an internal memo, "growth ideology" has no single founding document and no one author — economists across capitalist and state-socialist systems alike converged on it independently. But it did get a canonical text and an institutional home: a 1960 book gave it a five-stage doctrine, and the postwar Bretton Woods institutions — the IMF, the World Bank — operationalized it as loan conditionality across the developing world for decades. An economist who spent six years inside the World Bank arguing against exactly this resigned in 1994 rather than keep losing that argument from within. So even here, where the doctrine itself is genuinely closer to an emergent pattern than a single choice, its adoption as *policy* has real dates and institutions attached — the doctrine's canonical book, Bretton Woods lending, and the insider who was there watching it happen and said so.

None of these are humanity. Each was built, adopted, or defended by specific people and specific institutions — a philosopher's own metaphysics, a pope's chancery, a court, a parliament passing Enclosure Act after Enclosure Act, an economist inside his own report, a scientist inside his own company, a bank inside its own lending conditions. Say the generic "humanity" instead, and every one of them stays unnamed.

## A third trap, underneath the first two

Naming a doctrine doesn't indict everyone who shares a civilization with it. Name the dualist metaphysics and you haven't indicted every Western mind since — including the naturalists, mystics, and peasants who never accepted the frame, and the colonized cultures who were run over by enclosure rather than authors of it. Name the trade association's disinformation campaign and you haven't indicted the company scientist who did honest work in 1977 and was ignored by his own employer. The naming has to stay aimed at the doctrine, the instrument, the institution, and the decision — not slide sideways onto whoever's nearest and easiest to be angry at.

There's a second trap underneath that one, and it applies to this essay's own method. Naming "Exxon" or "the World Bank" doesn't reach bedrock — an institution is itself an aggregate, of executives who rotate through fiduciary roles, shareholders who include ordinary pension holders, regulators who permitted the extraction, and a public that wanted the cheap energy. Point at the institution and the diffusion problem this essay set out to solve reappears one level down, inside the name. A historian of settler colonialism named the reason precisely, in a 2006 article: settler colonialism is a *structure*, reproduced by the ordinary, lawful, everyday operation of property law and resource extraction — not a discrete event with a culpable cast list waiting to be looked up. A 2012 essay in decolonization studies, "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor," carries that argument forward; the structural claim itself belongs to the 2006 article.

That points to a distinction this essay had been dodging by treating every case above the same way. Some of the harm on the list is *event-shaped*: a bull signed in 1455, a briefing written in 1977, a report delivered to Congress in 1934 — a decision, made once, by someone who could have decided otherwise, and who is therefore a fair target for a name. Where an event-shaped harm has a decision-maker, the naming targets the role-and-decision: the chief executive who received the briefing and funded the doubt campaign, the signer of the bull, the committee that adopted the lending condition. But some harm is *structure-shaped*: reproduced every day by millions of ordinary, lawful, interchangeable acts — a mortgage signed, a commodity bought, a property line respected — where no single actor's individual choice was the hinge and no different individual choice would have mattered. A moral philosopher made the sharpest version of this case about an individual driver's emissions: there is no threshold any single tank of gas crosses. Treat structure-shaped harm as if it were event-shaped and you get scapegoating instead of accountability — a law professor's empirical study of U.S. corporate deferred-prosecution agreements between 2001 and 2014 has the numbers on this. Only about a third named any individual at all, and the individuals who were named skewed toward line staff, not the executives who set the incentives. Naming aimed at the wrong causal shape doesn't just fail to fix the harm. It hands the actual structure a scapegoat and lets it keep running underneath the name.

So the fix isn't "always find a person." It's matching the noun to the shape the harm actually has. Say the role and the decision where a decision was made. Where the harm is instead a standing structure, with no decision any single actor could have made differently, name the structure's mechanism instead — the rule, the incentive, the property form — rather than inventing an author it doesn't have. "Humanity" fails not because generality is always false. It fails because it borrows the vocabulary of a structural claim to do the work of an evasion — dissolving a locatable decision into an unlocatable one, on cases that were never structure-shaped to begin with. Every case in the list above is event-shaped. That is exactly why "humanity" was the wrong word for every one of them.

## What reparatory research actually looks like

Not an essay. A research program, with a bibliography, a method, and people already doing the work who deserve to be cited rather than reinvented:

**Trace the doctrine to its adoption points — where the harm is event-shaped.** Each mechanism above has a moment it entered law, curriculum, or policy — *Romanus Pontifex* in 1455, the 1934 national-income report to Congress, the 1960 growth doctrine taken up by Bretton Woods lending policy, the Australian High Court's 1992 terra nullius ruling. Those are archival facts: a court record, a congressional report, a bank's lending history. Follow them and you reach a document; follow the document and, for event-shaped harm, you reach the person who signed it. That's the whole promise of this method, and it only holds where the harm actually has an author. It cannot make the same promise for the structure-shaped harm named above — that needs a different kind of research, described next.

**Read the people who never consented to the frame.** The 2006 settler-colonialism article is the source of the structure/event distinction this essay leans on above; the 2012 essay "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" carries it forward into the argument that settler-colonial harm resists a culpable cast list. Alongside them: rights-of-nature law (Ecuador's 2008 constitution, the Whanganui River's 2017 legal personhood in Aotearoa New Zealand), traditional ecological knowledge as a body of empirical practice, land-back movements with actual legal theories behind them. This is not a gap that needs a Western thinker to fill. It's existing, rigorous work that the doctrine-naming above has spent centuries refusing to cite as authoritative — and citing it, here, is the least this piece owes it.

**Follow the money forward, not just the doctrine backward.** Climate liability litigation — states and cities suing fossil fuel companies using the same internal documents that trace back to the 1977 briefing — loss-and-damage funds negotiated at UN climate conferences, odious-debt frameworks applied to colonial-era extraction: these are places where "who owes what to whom" is being adjudicated in real institutions right now, however slowly, and however often the institution being sued turns out to be defended by the same diffusion this essay just described. That diffusion is the terrain of the fight, not a reason not to fight it.

**Hold the ledger, and match the remedy to the harm's shape.** The costs that were externalized have to be re-internalized somewhere, by someone, on purpose. For event-shaped harm, a true accounting names who profited, names what they profited from, and asks what repayment would concretely look like. For structure-shaped harm, the accounting has to target the mechanism instead — the rule that makes the harm self-renewing — because there is no name at the bottom of it to send the bill to. Diffuse guilt lets both questions stay permanently deferred by pretending they're the same question. They aren't, and naming which one you're asking is where the research actually starts.

## The shape of it

"Humanity" was never wrong because it was too big a word. It was wrong because it did a job — softening a specific indictment into an unaddressed one, on harm that had specific, locatable, event-shaped authors — while wearing the clothes of humility. The fix isn't a smaller word. It's the right-sized one: a role and a decision, for a choice someone made and could have made differently; a mechanism, for a structure that keeps making the decision for everyone, forever, on its own.

The harm reaches everyone. That part of "humanity" was always true, and stays true. The cause does not reach everyone equally, and pretending otherwise was never mercy — it was cover. Both of those sentences have to be held at once, or the whole thing collapses back into the blur it started as.

Say the role and the decision, where there is a decision to point to. Name the mechanism, where there isn't. Either way: stop saying humanity.
