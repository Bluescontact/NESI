## Communications discipline and legal protections

Every written communication going to the primary adverse party, to agencies, to relevant third parties, or to dispute-resolution intermediaries is governed by three doctrines. Honoring them keeps all communications protected and forecloses any defamation counter-claim. The templates in this kit already comply; this section names *why* so future communications stay in compliance.

### The three doctrines that protect what is being said

**1. Truth.** Truth is a complete defense to defamation. Every factual statement in the kit's communications must be documented by evidence in the record. List each load-bearing factual claim and its evidentiary source. Per-substrate kit fills in:

No factual statement should appear in any communication without a corresponding row above. If a row cannot be filled, the statement is not yet ripe for written use.

**2. Opinion / fair comment.** Legal characterizations — *"willful violation," "non-merchantable," "bad faith," "negligent," "discriminatory," "in breach"* — are opinions based on disclosed facts. Stating an opinion grounded in facts that have been disclosed in the same communication is protected under common law fair comment doctrine and First Amendment principles.

The protection requires that the underlying facts be disclosed. An opinion offered without the basis facts is not protected.

**3. Privilege.** Communications to government agencies in connection with potential or pending official action carry **qualified privilege** (some jurisdictions: absolute). Filings with regulators, AGs, licensing bodies, and any court complaint fall within this protection. Direct correspondence to an adverse party cannot defame that party about themselves (publication-to-third-party requirement). Communications to commercially interested third parties (manufacturers, franchisors, principals) enjoy **common-interest privilege** when both parties share a legitimate interest in the underlying conduct. Dispute-resolution intermediaries (card networks, insurance adjusters, mediators) receive privileged communications in the formal-dispute channel.

Per-substrate kit lists the specific recipients and their privilege basis:

| Recipient | Privilege basis |
|---|---|
| [Primary adverse party] | Not defamation — written to subject; cannot defame about themselves |
| [Regulator, e.g. State AG] | Qualified privilege — communication to enforcement agency |
| [Court — civil filing] | Absolute privilege — judicial pleading |
| [Manufacturer / franchisor] | Common-interest privilege — shared interest in dealer/franchisee conduct |
| [Dispute intermediary, e.g. card issuer] | Formal-dispute-channel privilege |

### Three rules of communication discipline

These keep the protections above intact. The templates honor them; future communications should too.

**Rule 1 — Conduct, not character.** Write about *the adverse party's conduct, response pattern, or correspondence,* not about any individual's character. The legal protections above evaporate when communications shift from documented conduct to character claims.

| Safe form | Unsafe form |
|---|---|
| "[Entity]'s conduct" | "[Individual] is dishonest" |
| "The seller's response pattern" | "[Individual] is a crook" |
| "[Entity] failed to respond to formal demand" | "[Individual] is avoiding accountability" |

**Rule 2 — Watch the legal terms of art.** Specific legal terms carry specific burdens. Using them gratuitously imports legal exposure you do not need.

Per-substrate kit fills in the watch-list for this matter. Common examples:

- **"Fraud"** — requires intent to deceive, reliance, damages with specific elements. Use only when you intend to prove all elements. If your actual claim is breach of warranty + UPA violation, do not use "fraud."
- **"Abandon"** (UCC revocation contexts) — creates a defense for the seller that revocation collapsed. Use *held at seller's disposition, parked, preserved* instead.
- **"Discriminate"** (employment / housing / public-accommodations contexts) — invokes specific civil rights statutes with elements you may not be claiming.
- **"Harassment"** — narrow legal meanings vary by jurisdiction; informal use can muddy or strengthen the claim depending on context.

The general principle: any word that names a legal cause of action should be used only when you are actually claiming that cause of action, with all its elements.

**Rule 3 — Hold public-facing communications in reserve.** Public statements (reviews, social posts, public consumer-protection sites, press) have different exposure than communications to specific parties with legitimate interest:

- Fewer privileges (no qualified privilege for general public broadcast)
- Larger audience (greater potential damages if anything is challenged)
- Can interfere with adjudication of pending formal-dispute or regulatory processes
- Can complicate settlement

Most demand letters reserve the right to consumer-facing communications. *Reserve* the right; do not exercise it until at least the primary formal-dispute process resolves. The leverage of *threatening* public communication is sometimes more valuable than the act itself. Reserve as escalation for late-phase use.
