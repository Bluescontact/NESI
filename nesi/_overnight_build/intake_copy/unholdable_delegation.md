# Unholdable Delegation

---

## The mechanism

Some tasks require the delegator's own substrate to be held — not their authority over the task, but their direct access to it: their proximity, their sensory contact with what is happening, their position as the one who bears the cost. Delegation moves a task without moving this substrate. The task is now in someone else's hands, but the substrate it requires stays with the delegator.

This is the unholdable variant: the delegate cannot hold the task not because they lack skill or willingness, but because holding it requires something the delegator still has. The task exceeds what can be held at the delegate's position.

---

## Why it surfaces as collapse, not refusal

Refusal requires naming what cannot be held. Naming requires that the delegate already sees the structure clearly enough to identify the unholdability. In most cases, they don't — the task arrives as a normal delegation, carried in the language of professional responsibility or reasonable expectation. The delegate accepts.

The collapse comes later: under load, under complexity, when the gap between what the task requires and what the delegate has access to becomes operational. What the delegator reads as failure or incompetence is the structural gap arriving as consequence.

The collapse maps where the substrate ends. The delegate fails at precisely the point the task required access they didn't have.

---

## Three instances

**The felt gate.** [[the_governor]] §2 names this directly: the builder cannot set the origin because the origin requires the body that bears the cost. Delegating the felt-gate to any agent without that body produces one of two outcomes — a closed gate that the closing body does not own (fake closure), or a permanently open gate because the delegate correctly refuses the impossible. Each instrument ships with one open `[KEVIN]` calibration zero precisely because that zero cannot be set from outside.

**Safety compliance through a chain without enforcement reach.** A general contractor delegates safety compliance to a subcontractor who has no authority to stop work when conditions are violated. The subcontractor accepts because the framing is liability-shifted, not structurally assessed. The collapse is not the subcontractor's failure; it is the structural gap between the task (stopping unsafe work) and the position's actual reach (noting it, flagging it, hoping it changes).

**Diagnostic authority without system access.** A consultant is delegated the problem of naming what is wrong in a system they cannot observe directly. The substrate of diagnosis — direct access to what is happening — stays with the people inside. The consultant accepts. The resulting diagnosis names what is visible at their level of access. Substrate-level gaps remain unnamed because the consultant cannot reach them.

---

## The recognition

Three conditions together mark the pattern:

1. The collapse happens at a specific point, not generally — the delegate functioned up to the exact moment the task required access they lacked.
2. The collapse is repeatable — a different delegate placed in the same position produces the same failure at the same point.
3. The delegator still has the substrate the task requires — it was never transferred.

Condition 3 distinguishes this from simple incapacity. Simple incapacity: the delegate couldn't do this task. Unholdable delegation: no delegate could do this task, with this authority, in this position. The task is not defeasible to the delegate's failure.

---

## Edges

This pattern is structurally close to [[cost_externalized_through_delegation]] but the mechanism differs. Cost externalization: authority/cost are decoupled — the task IS holdable, but the cost is absorbed by someone other than the authority-holder. Unholdable delegation: the task itself requires what stays with the delegator. The two co-occur when cost is externalized precisely because the task is unholdable, making the collapse invisible until it arrives.

The pattern suggests a test before delegation: *does this task require substrate that will not transfer with the work?* If yes, delegation produces one of three outcomes — fake closure (the delegate pretends), collapse (the delegate accepts and fails), or honest naming (the delegate surfaces the unholdability). Only the third is not a failure mode.
