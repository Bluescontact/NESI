## Transferable form

A delegator transfers a task while retaining the conditions that guarantee its failure — not from distrust of the receiver, but because each failure cycle re-proves that the delegator alone can hold the thing. The function is not control; it is the periodic renewal of necessity. The receiver's capability is structurally irrelevant: the loop closes regardless.

## Mechanism

The mechanism runs in three phases that repeat without natural termination.

**Phase 1 — Delegation with withheld substrate.** The delegator hands the task forward but retains one or more load-bearing conditions: context, authority, access, relationship capital, or the criteria by which success would be recognized. The withholding is rarely conscious. It manifests as "I'll stay close in case you need me" or structural inaccessibility of the thing that would make the catcher sufficient.

**Phase 2 — Predictable failure.** The catcher cannot close the loop because the loop was never fully transferred. The failure arrives as evidence — to both parties — that the task required the delegator. The catcher's capability is not tested; what is tested is whether they can succeed without the withheld substrate. They cannot. This is not a capability finding.

**Phase 3 — Re-entry as rescue.** The delegator steps back in. The re-entry is framed (sincerely) as support, competence, or care. It functions as indispensability proof. The delegator's necessity is renewed. The catcher learns that full ownership is not available to them. The delegator learns that handing off doesn't work.

The loop then resets.

What distinguishes this pattern from ordinary micromanagement: the delegator is not monitoring the catcher's execution. They are, often unconsciously, monitoring for the moment the loop needs closing — which their own structure has already guaranteed will arrive. The behavior looks like care or competence. It functions as self-renewal. The affect (distrust, anxiety, high standards) is real and is not the mechanism. The mechanism is structural: indispensability cannot be proved in a single cycle; it must be renewed. Each cycle is the proof.

## Failure modes

- Competent catchers exit the system (they recognize the loop before the delegator does).
- Less competent catchers remain and confirm the delegator's read that only they can hold it.
- The delegator's internal model solidifies: genuine attempts to delegate feel like risk rather than transfer.
- Organizational or relational load concentrates upward and does not distribute, regardless of stated intent.

## Falsifier

If the withheld substrate is named and transferred in full — including the criteria by which success would be recognized — and the catcher then closes the loop without the delegator's re-entry, the loop is broken for that cycle. Repeated across cycles, this falsifies the pattern for that dyad. The harder version of the falsifier: if the delegator cannot identify what they withheld until after they re-enter, the loop is not correctable through awareness alone (see Edges).

## Edges

The hardest open question: is the withheld substrate always identifiable before the failure arrives, or does the delegator only know what they withheld after they re-enter? If the latter, the loop is not correctable through awareness — the delegator cannot see the withholding in advance because their model of the task is the thing that fills what should have been transferred.

Second tension: the loop can produce genuinely good outcomes. If the delegator is in fact more capable in this specific domain, their re-entry may produce better results than full transfer would have. The pattern is real and the indispensability may be real simultaneously. Whether the loop is generative (builds the catcher's capacity over time) or extractive (concentrates it permanently) is not always a clean line.

Third edge: what breaks the loop? Forcing full transfer is the structural answer, but it requires the delegator to name what they are withholding — which demands they recognize the mechanism while inside it. The more common break is catcher exit, not delegator insight. This points toward a receiving-side version of the pattern that has not been developed: what does a catcher do when they can identify the withheld substrate and the delegator cannot?

What the pattern does not yet reach: the organizational scale, where the "delegator" may be distributed — a culture, a role-structure, a legacy system — that withholds substrate not through any individual's intent but through accumulated architecture. The mechanism may be identical; the intervention surface is entirely different.
