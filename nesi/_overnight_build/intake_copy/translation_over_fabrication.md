## Transferable form

When a vision exceeds available capacity, the highest-value offer is not production but translation — converting the vision into a form a builder could build from. This is a definitional move, not a delivery move. The translator does not build the thing; the translator makes the thing buildable.

## Mechanism

Fabrication requires capacity alignment between vision and builder; translation requires only clarity alignment between vision and definition. These are different operations with different prerequisites. Fabrication can fail for lack of resources, time, skill, or scope. Translation can succeed with none of those — it fails only for lack of precision.

Three phases: vision arrives overloaded — desire, ambiguity, implicit constraints, and unstated success criteria all fused together. The translator disaggregates the load: separates what-it-is from what-it-does from what-would-prove-it-works. The output is not the thing but a specification a builder can contract against — which may reveal the original vision was not buildable as stated, or was already built and unrecognized, or was two different things needing separation before either could be built.

The offer structure: not "I will build this" and not "I cannot help you." The offer is "I can help you determine whether this can be built and into what form it needs to be in for a builder to take it." This is a bounded, completable engagement — it ends when definition is reached, not when construction is complete. The translator is not on the hook for what the builder does with the definition.

The definitional move is the value. A builder handed a clear definition can build. A builder handed an overloaded vision must translate before building — paying the translation cost whether or not paid for it, and often building the wrong thing. Translation pulled forward removes that cost from every downstream actor.

## Failure modes

1. **Translator builds instead of translates** — absorbing the capacity gap by attempting to compensate for it rather than naming it. Burns the translator's credibility (delivered something mismatched) and the client's time (received something unspecified).
2. **Reception mishears the offer** — the client hears "help getting to a definition" as a lesser offer than "building," interpreting translation as deferral rather than as the actual service. The translator must name the distinction clearly or the gift does not land.

## Resolution of the role-boundary edges (re-screen, 2026-07-15)

Translation wins when the blocker is definitional ambiguity (the vision-holder doesn't yet know exactly what they want built). Fabrication wins anyway when the blocker is a resource/skill gap and the vision-holder already has full clarity — there, offering translation instead of building is a stall, not a service.

Real-translation-vs-thin-deliverable test: does the output let an independent third-party builder produce the thing without further contact with the original vision-holder? A thin deliverable still needs the vision-holder present to interpret it; a real translation is complete enough for a stranger to execute from alone.

## Edges

- Assumes translation is separable from fabrication — but in domains where the act of building is the only way to arrive at a definition (exploratory prototyping, improvisational creative work, live fieldwork), translation-before-fabrication may be a false upstream; the translation loop runs inside the fabrication loop. Where is that boundary?
- The offer requires the client to trust that definition is valuable — the translator carries the burden of demonstrating the value of definitional clarity before it exists, a separate competency from the translation itself.
- Does not close what happens when the translator and the builder are the same person across time — the governor between the two loops (the moment of "is this now buildable?") can be lost, collapsed, or skipped.
