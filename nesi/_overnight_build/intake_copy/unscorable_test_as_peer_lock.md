## Transferable form

When a task is handed off without a stated success condition, the receiver cannot satisfy it — not because they lack competence, but because the scoring instrument does not exist. The absence of a definition is not neutral: it functions as a structural lock that prevents the receiver from ever demonstrating sufficiency and thereby rising to peer standing. Incompetence and excellence produce the same result.

## Mechanism

The mechanism has two components that compound: (1) undefined success criteria and (2) a standing-gate that can only open through demonstrated sufficiency.

Without a stated success condition, evaluation is not suspended — it defaults to the giver's implicit standard, which remains private and malleable. The giver retains the ability to move the threshold retroactively. This is not necessarily intentional; the threshold may simply never have been formed. The effect is identical either way: the receiver performs against a criterion that does not exist in any form they can access.

The standing-gate operates separately. Peer standing is not conferred by time served or by the giver's satisfaction — it requires the receiver to demonstrate that they have met the bar the giver set. If that bar was never stated, it cannot be met. The receiver cannot appeal to their performance record because no performance can count as pass when no test was specified.

This means the structure locks the receiver below the giver regardless of competence trajectory. A highly skilled receiver who executes flawlessly is indistinguishable from a mediocre one, structurally: neither has cleared a threshold, because the threshold was never placed.

The peer-lock is not a temporary condition awaiting correction. Each cycle of unscored performance reinforces the asymmetry: the giver accumulates the right to evaluate; the receiver accumulates only effort. The roles calcify.

## Failure modes

- The receiver infers a success condition and optimizes for it. The giver's private standard diverges. The receiver believes they are succeeding; the giver's evaluation tracks a different axis. The gap compounds silently.
- The receiver escalates effort, reading the absence of praise as insufficient performance rather than as the absence of a scoreable test. This produces overwork against an unmovable ceiling.
- The giver interprets the receiver's continued asking-for-feedback as evidence of inadequacy, confirming the asymmetry from the giver's position.
- The lock becomes deniable. If challenged, the giver can offer any post-hoc standard as if it was always the criterion. The receiver has no prior statement to hold against it.

## Falsifier

If a giver, asked directly to name the success condition in writing, does so — and a receiver's performance against that named standard is then acknowledged as sufficient (peer standing granted) — the lock is not operating for that instance. If the giver instead cannot or will not name a standard, or names one only to move it after it is met, the lock is confirmed.

## Edges

The pattern assumes the standing-gate is real — that peer status actually depends on demonstrated sufficiency. In some structures, peer standing is conferred through other channels (tenure, title, social recognition) that bypass the task-performance circuit entirely. The pattern needs to specify when the standing-gate is the operative mechanism vs. when other gates are in play.

It is unclear whether the unscorable condition must be intentional to function as a lock. The structural analysis holds regardless of intent, but the remediation differs sharply: an unconscious omission can be corrected by asking for criteria; a deliberate withholding cannot. The pattern does not yet distinguish these cases or name what evidence would let a receiver tell them apart.

The pattern does not address the duration threshold — at what point does the absence of a success condition become constitutive of the relationship rather than a correctable gap? Early in a task, unstated criteria are common and often harmless. The lock only fully closes once the receiver has invested enough that leaving would be a loss, and the giver knows it. The timing of that threshold is uncharted here.

What the pattern points toward but does not reach: a theory of scoreable handoffs — what the minimum sufficient specification of a success condition looks like, and who bears the cost of building it when neither party has done so.
