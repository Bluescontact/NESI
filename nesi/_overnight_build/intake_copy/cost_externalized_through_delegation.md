# Cost Externalized Through Delegation

---

## The mechanism

Authority is positional — it travels with the title, the contract, the ownership stake. Cost is physical — it travels with proximity, presence, and attention. Delegation moves proximity without moving authority. The result is systematic decoupling: the delegate absorbs cognitive burden (planning, contingency, sense-making), logistical burden (sequencing, coordination, error-correction), and liability burden (being there when things go wrong) while the authority-holder retains the right to override, redirect, or terminate.

The gap is self-reinforcing once established. The authority-holder's model of the system drifts from reality because feedback must travel through the delegate, who filters, buffers, and translates. The gap between who bears cost and who holds power is the externalization — and it is structurally stable until the system fails catastrophically or proximity is forced.

---

## The hidden subsidy

The authority-holder's decision-making is cheaper than it appears, because the delegate has pre-processed the situation. The delegate's decision-making is more expensive than it appears, because it operates under constraint — any move that approaches the authority boundary requires permission or risks overreach.

The externalization is mutually legible as something else — "trust," "autonomy," "professional development" — framings that rename the cost-transfer as a benefit to the delegate. The structural effect is identical regardless of intent.

---

## Three failure modes

**Information lag:** the authority-holder's model of the system drifts from reality. Feedback must travel through the delegate, who filters and translates. Crisis signals cross the gap (catastrophe has visible costs); competence signals don't (the unrecorded ordinary never does). The authority-holder receives alarms but not the steady state that would have prevented them.

**Accountability inversion:** when the system fails, the delegate bears proximate blame (they were there) while the authority-holder retains structural distance (they delegated). Responsibility migrates in the opposite direction from authority.

**Exit asymmetry:** the delegate can exit by quitting; the authority-holder can exit by re-delegating. The cost never returns to the authority-holder unless delegation entirely collapses and proximity is forced.

---

## What breaks it

Two moves close the gap: the agent exits the delegation structure, or the authority-holder moves back into operational proximity. Both are structurally equivalent — they reunite authority and cost-bearing in the same body. Only then does the feedback path restore.

The brake alone (the Governor's STRUCTURAL SOURCE return) correctly names the condition and cannot fix it. Calibrating intake when cost is being structurally generated upstream is treating a structural problem as a somatic one.

---

## Edges

What happens when authority becomes nominal — the absentee owner who has lost the thread — while the delegate holds de facto power while still bearing de facto cost? This may be a distinct failure mode or a terminal state of the same one.

At what point does accumulated cost-bearing become leverage? Delegates who absorb enough cost sometimes acquire informal authority precisely because they are the only ones with an accurate system model. The externalization may contain its own reversal mechanism.

The pattern is clearest in employment and ownership structures, but the same geometry appears in emotional labor, care work, and institutional knowledge — domains where proximity-cost is even less legible as cost. Whether the mechanism runs identically in relational rather than operational proximity is genuinely open.
