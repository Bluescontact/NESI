## Transferable form

When a success condition is withheld, the absence is not an oversight but a mechanism: a defined target, once met, triggers an obligation the delegator is unwilling to honor. The undefinition keeps that obligation permanently deferred. The delegated work is not failing to find a standard — it is succeeding at producing a deficit that the structure requires in order to function.

## Mechanism

The mechanism operates in three interlocking moves.

**First:** the delegator holds the success condition off the table. Not through negligence — the withholder knows what would constitute success. The knowing is the condition of the withholding. A standard they could not name would not be useful to them. The standard they refuse to name is extremely useful.

**Second:** the absence of a standard converts every performance into a proof of insufficiency. Any output the delegate produces becomes readable as falling short, because there is no fixed bar against which it could count as clearance. The evaluative field is permanently tilted. This is not drift — it is architecture.

**Third:** the failure of the delegated work delivers a specific payload: the thing has to return to the delegator. "See, it falls back to me." The return is not a consolation; it is the point. The structure manufactures the deficit it appears to discover. Indispensability is not threatened by the delegate's competence because the measurement instrument is under the delegator's control.

Structural truth: the deficit is not a byproduct. It is the deliverable. What looks like a management failure is a management success measured against the actual objective.

## Failure modes

1. **Mis-diagnosed as incompetence.** It can be mis-read as simple incompetence of the delegator, which allows the structure to remain intact while attracting sympathy rather than scrutiny.
2. **Forced legibility collapses it.** A highly capable delegate who forces legibility — who names the missing standard in writing and requests ratification — can collapse the mechanism. The withholder must then either name a standard (triggering the obligation) or refuse to name one (making the withholding visible). Most withholders avoid both by escalating ambiguity or moving the scope.
3. **Self-concealing.** Because the stated reason for failure is always the task's difficulty or the delegate's limitation, the structure never has to announce itself. The cover story is built into the failure.

## Falsifier

Name the condition. If the delegator, when asked to state a concrete success standard in writing, accepts and ratifies one — and the mechanism dissolves (performance can now clear a bar) — the pattern does not apply to that instance. If the delegator instead escalates ambiguity or relocates scope rather than naming a standard, the pattern is confirmed.

## Edges

The pattern assumes the delegator is operating consciously — that the withholding is intentional. But most instances of this structure probably run below deliberate choice: the delegator may have internalized the mechanism without having named it, which means calling it intentional may misread the phenomenology even when the structure is accurate. Whether a structural move requires conscious intent to be culpable is unresolved.

A second open edge: institutional contexts can produce this mechanism without any single actor choosing it — the undefinition distributed across layers of authority, each of which would claim it cannot define the standard because that belongs to another layer. Whether this is the same pattern or a homologous one with different leverage points is not settled.

Third: what allows a delegate to exit rather than only collapse the mechanism? The analysis identifies how the structure can be made visible, but visibility is not always exit. Some delegates cannot exit even when they see it clearly. The relationship between structural legibility and the delegate's actual freedom to leave is a gap this pattern does not close.
