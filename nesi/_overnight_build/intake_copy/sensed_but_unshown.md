# sensed_but_unshown

**The pattern in one line.** A cost is stable in the body of the party who absorbed it and invisible in the accounting of the system that generated it — not by oversight, but because no field exists for that cost type in the generating system's ledger; and the stability of an externalization is proportional to the non-representability of the harm in the system responsible for it.

*Extends [[cost_externalized_through_delegation]] at the representability axis. The parent names the mechanism of decoupling: delegation moves proximity without authority, and the cost migrates to whoever is nearest. This adds the structural feature that determines whether the externalization is reversible or permanent: whether the cost type can be entered into the generating system's own accounting. If it can — injury claim, damages, measurable output loss — it surfaces as a line item and can generate redress. If it cannot — reputation, future-work foreclosed, standing degraded, the body's accumulated load — it never appears in the system that produced it, and the externalization is structurally stable. The parent names where cost goes; this names what determines whether it can return.*

---

## The mechanism

A harm has two faces simultaneously:

The **felt face** — how it registers in the body and standing of the party who absorbs it. This face is real, often precise, and can be held with high fidelity by the person who bore the cost. The body knows what it cost.

The **shown face** — how it can be entered into the accounting of the system that generated it. This face is a function of the ledger's field structure: what cost types the system can record, measure, aggregate, and act on.

The gap between these two faces is the load-bearing structure. When a cost type has no field in the generating system's ledger — no metric column, no claim type, no record category — the harm is **sensed but unshown**: real to the absorber, absent from the system that produced it.

This is not an information problem. The generating system is not unaware that cost occurred; it is structurally incapable of representing it. The field does not exist, which means no amount of signal from the absorber can make the cost legible to the system's own accounting. The harm cannot be entered, not because no one is listening, but because there is no column for it.

---

## Why non-representability stabilizes the externalization

A representable cost creates pressure for correction. An injury claim, a measurable output loss, a financial write-down — these appear as line items in the generating system's own ledger and trigger the system's own correction mechanisms: liability, insurance, renegotiation, adjustment. The externalization is visible from inside the system and the system has instruments to respond to it.

A non-representable cost creates no such pressure. The harm is real; the ledger is silent. The generating system's instruments do not fire because there is nothing for them to read. From inside the generating system, the cost does not exist — not because it was hidden, but because it was never the kind of thing the ledger could hold. The externalization is structurally stable because the system that produced it has no mechanism that can detect it.

This is why certain externalized costs are permanent where others are reversible. A cost that can be represented can be contested, compensated, aggregated into policy change, or surfaced in audit. A cost that cannot be represented can only be borne, absorbed, distributed through informal networks, or eventually written into the body as damage that predates any specific cause. The non-representability is the condition that converts a temporary externalization into a permanent one.

---

## The specific cost types that cannot cross the ledger boundary

The pattern is sharpest for costs that are:

**Non-monetary by nature.** Future-work foreclosed, standing degraded in a network, reputation altered, social position shifted — these do not reduce to a number the ledger can sum. The harm is real and often large; the number is unavailable.

**Relational and non-transferable.** The cost of a broken working relationship, an irreversibly altered trust, a foreclosed collaboration — these live in the space between parties and cannot be atomized into a line item belonging to one.

**Accumulated and baseline-shifting.** The accumulated metabolic cost of chronic overload, the gradual degradation of a body that has been absorbing cost for years — there is no event that marks when the cost was incurred, no invoice, no moment of transfer. The harm registers only in the changed baseline, which is not a cost in the ledger; it is the current state.

**Unverifiable from outside the body.** The costs the generating system most needs to deny — the load the delegate carries, the fear the worker performs around, the calculation the subordinate makes before speaking — are precisely the costs for which the absorber's testimony is the only evidence, and the absorber's testimony is precisely what the generating system has no mechanism to accept as a cost type.

---

## The design implication

Systems designed to externalize non-representable costs are not defective in their accounting — they are functioning as designed. The ledger correctly reflects all costs of the type the ledger was built to hold. The non-representability is often not an oversight but a structural feature: including the cost type would make visible a pattern of externalization the system's operators have no interest in seeing.

The design move, when constructing a system that claims to account for cost, is to ask: **what cost types are absorbed by parties adjacent to this system that cannot be entered into this system's ledger?** The answer describes the externalized harm load. A system that cannot answer this question has not failed to measure — it has succeeded in not building the field.

This is the fourth move in the remedy-design-moves cluster:
- Bias the direction of failure — *choose which way harm lands when it cannot be prevented*
- Structural incapacity over prohibition — *make harm unrepresentable by a correctly-operating system*
- Extend, don't invent — *set authority at its honest height*
- Sensed but unshown — *name the cost type that the generating system's ledger cannot hold*

The four moves together constitute a grammar for designing systems where harm and its redress are structurally coupled rather than structurally severed.

## Failure modes

**Mistaking representable proxy for felt cost.** A system adds a metric for something adjacent to the harm (reported incidents, complaint forms filed, turnover rate) and treats the proxy as the cost. The metric captures the representable tip; the felt harm continues accumulating beneath it. The system registers improvement in the proxy while the actual cost load increases. The proxy becomes the definition of the cost, and the definition is chosen precisely because it is measurable, not because it is accurate.

**Treating testimony as the ledger entry.** A system with no field for non-representable cost accepts testimony from absorbers as a substitute. Testimony is real; testimony is not a ledger entry. Without a field, testimony produces empathy (or not) and then dissolves — it does not become a line item that aggregates, generates policy pressure, or appears in audit. The testimony enters through a door with no room behind it.

**Non-representability as designed feature, narrated as limitation.** The generating system presents its inability to represent certain costs as a technical limitation ("we don't have a way to measure that") rather than a structural choice ("we built a ledger that cannot hold that"). The narration of limitation suppresses the recognition that a different ledger was available and not chosen.

---

## The falsifier

If the cost type could be entered into the generating system's accounting — a field existed, a metric was tracked, a claim type was recognized — the externalization would be visible from inside the system and subject to the system's correction mechanisms. The stability of the externalization depends on the absence of the field. The test: **build the field and see whether the cost becomes visible**. If it does, the non-representability was structural. If it doesn't — if the cost still cannot be captured even with a field — the harm lives in a register that no field can reach, and this is a different and harder problem.

---

## Edges

The pattern assumes the felt cost is real and the unshown status is a property of the generating system's ledger. But the absorber's felt cost is also subject to error — accumulated grievance, attribution bias, category error. The sensed face is not verified simply by being felt. The pattern does not provide a method for distinguishing genuine non-representable harm from harm that is non-representable because it is misdescribed. The falsifier helps; it does not fully close this.

The design implication requires an actor positioned to ask honestly. The generating system's operators may be incapable of asking this question in good faith, not because they are dishonest but because they cannot see what their ledger structure excludes — the missing field is also missing from their perception of what is missing. The question requires standing outside the ledger you built, which is the hardest position from which to see.

The four-move grammar does not yet name what to do when all four moves have been applied and a cost remains non-representable. The grammar maximizes the scope of representable harm; it does not reach the harm that no field can hold — the kind that lives in felt reality and has no structural form. That residue may be permanent, and the pattern points at it without reaching it.
