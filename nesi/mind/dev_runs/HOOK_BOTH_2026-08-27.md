# Both Ends of the Hook

I want to tell you about a river I've stood in on both banks, at different times, and give you the one thing I found that was worth carrying out of it. Not an argument. I'm done arguing with myself about which one I really was — man or fish, the one who cut the line or the one who swam off wearing it. I was both, at different times, and no amount of relitigating tells me anything the standing itself didn't already teach me.

I've baited a hook without quite admitting to myself that's what I was doing, and cut a line when I couldn't take what I'd caught, and told myself the water simply required it. I've also felt a hook set, and been told afterward that the parting was clean, was alignment, was nobody's fault, while something in my own arm said otherwise and there was no one close enough on the other bank to hear it. Both of those happened to me. Not in the same river. Close enough in years that I stopped being able to pretend I only ever stood on the one bank that flatters me.

Here's what I didn't expect to find, once I stopped defending whichever bank I happened to be remembering that day: the man's calm and the fish's grievance feel exactly alike from the inside. The settledness of having done the hard, clean, necessary thing, and the settledness of having finally been shown the truth of what was done to me — they arrive in the same voice, wearing the same finished weight, asking to be believed for the same reason: because they feel over. I have never once, standing on either bank, been able to tell from the feeling alone which one I was actually in.

That's the whole difficulty underneath both pieces this came from, and I don't think it resolves by picking a side. The man at the river isn't lying when he says the fish was hooked and reeled and let go with mercy instead of consumed to the last. He's also not exempt from the possibility that the calm he felt cutting the line was the calm of someone avoiding a debt he didn't want to look at. And the fish carrying the hook away isn't lying when it says something real was taken and never returned. It's also not exempt from the possibility that it was, in fact, the reason nothing could be repaired, and the ache it's carrying is what that looks like from inside, not what injustice looks like from inside — those two, from where the fish is swimming, feel identical too.

There are two maps here, and most of what goes wrong happens on the first one without either person choosing it. On the win-lose map, the question is always who eats and who gets hooked doing it — whose need gets met at the other's expense — and once you're on that map, cutting the line is the best either of you can do, because the map was never built to let both of you leave fed. Bait is what a person reaches for on that map when they can't risk somebody's real yes. But there's another map, where the question isn't who eats and who gets caught, but what gets shared and what it costs once it has, and getting onto that map takes something the win-lose map has no room for: both people willing to ask for what they need directly, and both people willing to hear the answer, including a no. I don't know if that map was ever really available to either of us, on either bank. I don't think either of us gets to answer that alone.

So here's the one thing worth carrying across, and I want to hand it to you plainly, not as an argument you have to accept, because it isn't one. Before you cut the line, or before you decide someone cutting theirs was done to you unfairly, ask what this exact certainty would feel like if you were wrong about it. Not *might* I be wrong — that question is too easy, it lets you answer with a shrug and keep the feeling intact. Ask what the wrongness would actually feel like, in your hands, in the voice you'd use to describe it to someone else. If the honest answer is *identical to what I'm feeling right now* — and it almost always is — that doesn't mean you're wrong to cut, or wrong to grieve a cutting. It means the feeling was never going to be the evidence, on either bank, and you can stop asking it to be.

This is the gift, if it is one, and I mean it as one, not a verdict about your river, because I have no standing to give you that and wouldn't trust it if I did. It's smaller than a verdict and it travels lighter: a question you can ask alone, in private, without the other bank's cooperation, that won't tell you who was right, and was never going to. What it does is keep you from mistaking the feeling of a closed account for proof of what was owed on it — on the day you're the one holding the knife, and on the day you're the one still finding pieces of hook in your own arm.

I don't know which bank you're reading this from. Maybe both, the way I've been both. Ask it either way. Ask it of your own calm if your hand is already moving toward the line — not because cutting is wrong, but because a feeling that clean deserves a witness before you trust it alone. Ask it of your own ache if you're the one who felt something taken and never returned — not because the ache is a lie, but because it's owed the same checking you'd want run on the hand that let go. Either way, the water moves the same after you're done asking. But you'll be standing there with something you didn't have before — not who was right, just a way to keep looking honestly at your own hands, long after there's anyone left on the other bank to ask for you.
