# What It Costs to Be Let Off the Hook

There's an ending I've come to recognize from the other side of it. Something goes wrong between me and someone else, and instead of the fight I was braced for, or the apology I was owed, or even the silence of someone still deciding — I get a verdict. Delivered once, not negotiated. Called a boundary. Called a statement of alignment. I don't get a vote on the word, and I don't get a vote on the sentence. I find out what happened to us the way I'd find out about weather: after the fact, from outside it, with nothing I can do about the front that already moved through.

I want to be careful here, because I know what this can turn into if I let it, and I've watched other people let it. This isn't going to be the story where I was the fish and someone else was the man with the hook. I don't actually know which one I was. That's the whole difficulty, and I'm not going to solve it by choosing the reading that makes me the injured party, just because it's the one available from where I'm standing.

## What "Statement of Alignment" Sounds Like From Here

I understand the appeal of that phrase, from the side that gets to say it. It sounds like something offered rather than something withheld — a value stated instead of a grievance filed. From here it sounds like a verdict handed down by a court I was never summoned to. There was a decision, made somewhere I don't have access to, and then there was the result, which arrived at my door looking exactly like weather.

Here's the part that's hard to say without sounding bitter, so I'll say it plainly instead: a boundary announced to me is a boundary. A boundary decided about me, somewhere I wasn't in the room, dressed afterward in the language of alignment so it doesn't have to sound like what it is — that's something else, and the vocabulary doesn't change which one happened. I don't get to tell, from my side, which one I got. Neither, I suspect, does the person who gave it to me. That's not an accusation. It's the same blindness the other piece names, just facing the other direction — and it comes with its own version of the same trap, which I'll get to.

## The Sentence That Isn't Mine Either

There's a line from the other side of this that I believe: "I closed it" and "it's closed" aren't the same sentence, and the second one isn't only the closer's to establish. I want to push that one step further than it usually gets pushed, because the step matters more from here than from there.

If it's not only theirs to establish, it's not only theirs to *revise*, either. Revision is exactly what happens on that side, quietly, over years — the doubt, the "I don't know yet" that gets asked and re-asked in private, at their own pace, with nobody checking the answer. I don't get that instrument. I got one delivery, and then silence where an appeals process would be. Whatever they work out about whether it was earned, they work out alone, and I never see the workings — only whatever they decide to hand back, if they ever decide to hand anything back at all.

## Leverage, From the Other End

I've read the version of this that admits closing an account is "usually available to whoever already has less to lose from the rupture, or holds the exit," and that this isn't rank-neutral. I want to sit with that longer than the version I read it in sat with it, because from here it isn't a confession. It's a description of what happened to me.

Somebody had an exit and I didn't, or had less of one, or leaving cost me something staying didn't cost them. That's not a coincidence sitting next to the decision — it's most of the explanation for it. The people who get to close an account cleanly are disproportionately the people who could already afford to leave. I'm not saying every closed account is an abuse of that asymmetry. I'm saying the asymmetry does more of the work than the vocabulary of alignment wants to admit, and I notice the other piece names this and keeps going, as if naming it were the same as answering it. To its credit, it says so about itself too, near the end — that noticing a thing once doesn't cover what gets built on top of it after. I'm holding it to that same standard here, from the side that doesn't get to write the account down anywhere.

## What I Can't Rule Out, Either

Here's where I have to be honest in the same direction, because it's the only way this doesn't turn into what I said at the start I didn't want it to turn into — and because the thing I'm about to say back to myself is the same thing the other piece eventually says to itself, just approached from a different bank.

I don't get to assume I was the fish. I might have been the man at the river and just never felt the hook, because it wasn't in my arm. It's possible I was the rupture, and the boundary was accurate, and the silence was the most generous thing available to someone who'd already tried the alternative and watched it fail. It's possible the story I'm telling right now — clean, no villain except the one holding the exit, calm and settled and free of my own culpability — is exactly the story a person tells when they were, in fact, the source of it and have decided not to look too hard at that.

And here's the part I don't think gets said often enough from this side of the water: my own grievance isn't neutral evidence either. It arrives in exactly the same clean, righteous shape whether I was genuinely cut off unfairly, or whether I was the reason no repair was coming and this is what that feels like from inside denial. Being denied a vote is real either way. It doesn't, on its own, tell me which kind of denial I actually got. So the same question the closer owes themselves, I owe myself too, aimed the other direction: *if I was, in fact, the rupture, what would this same certainty feel like from inside?* The honest answer is: identical to how it feels right now. That doesn't erase the ache. It means the ache was never going to be the evidence, on this side of the door any more than on the other.

## What I'm Not Going to Do With This

I'm not going to build a case out of this. I know what that costs, because I've watched people do it to the person who left them — replaying the ledger so every entry favors their own side, turning a real and complicated ending into a story that only needs one more retelling to become completely true. That's its own hook, and I don't want to be the one holding this line either.

So here's what I'll actually keep, and it's smaller than a verdict on anyone: whatever happened here, I wasn't in the room for the part where it was decided. That's true regardless of who was right. If I ever do this to someone else — and I will, eventually, because everyone eventually holds the exit in some relationship or other — I want to remember what it felt like to find out about my own ending as if it were weather. Not so I flinch away from ever closing an account again; some of them need closing, and I believe that as much as the piece arguing for it does. Just so that when I do it, I ask myself the harder question before I act, not the easier one that flatters me — and so I remember there's someone on the other side of the door who deserves at least the knowledge that a decision was made, even if I never owe them the vote.
